Known limits

The bridge is federated

Section titled “The bridge is federated”

Kaspa cannot verify Ethereum or BNB Chain, and they cannot verify Kaspa. In v1 a federation of 5 signers attests to deposits and burns, and 3 of them suffice. The threat model shows what a bad quorum could do; rate limits on both sides cap it.

Upgrade path: zero-knowledge light-client verification. Kaspa’s Toccata upgrade includes an opcode that verifies zero-knowledge proofs (OpZkPrecompile, KIP-16), and KIP-17 lists trustless bridges built on it as a use case. A future version could mint on Kaspa against a proof of the deposit, checked on-chain, instead of signer signatures. This is the planned direction, not part of v1, and it has no date.

It would cover one direction only. A proof checked on Kaspa replaces the signers’ word about deposits, so it removes the risk of unbacked mints. Releases are the other direction: the vaults on Ethereum and BNB Chain would have to verify Kaspa burns themselves, which needs a Kaspa light client on the EVM side, a separate and harder project. Until then, a release rests on 3 of 5 signers, capped by each vault’s release allowance (250 000 USDT at once, under 750 000 in any 24 hours). Every payout waits in the vault first, and the monitor pauses the vaults the moment it sees one queued with no burn behind it.

A reorg that flips a mint and a skip

Section titled “A reorg that flips a mint and a skip”

A Kaspa reorg can drop the block that accepted a mint, or a skip, and accept the other in its place. The signers, the coordinator and the monitor each keep a journal of the minter’s last 256 moves and undo the ones the dropped blocks accepted, by themselves, and then follow the new chain. (At first only the signers did. The coordinator has done so since 8 November and the monitor since rc.13, and this page went on saying otherwise until round 12: an operator was told to rebuild the coordinator and the monitor from a checkpoint after such a reorg. That is not needed, and a monitor rebuilt from a checkpoint has limits of its own.)

What is left: a reorg that undoes more than those 256 moves of the minter at once. A follower cannot undo past its journal. It then stays on a minter the chain no longer has, and fails safe: the monitor raises minter-lost and pauses, and no signer signs a refund from such a view. An operator brings it back from a recent checkpoint. The coordinator waits for each mint to be accepted before it sends the next, so by the project’s own arithmetic 256 moves are several minutes of chain at the least: a reorg that deep is deeper than the 1,000 DAA a burn waits before it is paid. None has been seen, and none was produced in a test.

A refund the monitor judges by its skip’s depth, as a release by its burn’s (since round 13, R13-ONCE-1: before, the skip’s presence at any depth was enough, and a queued refund was no longer watched). It asks the depth an honest refund of that amount always has, less 650 DAA for its own node’s lag. The signers wait longer since round 14 (the deepest tier for every refund), and the monitor still asks only the depth by amount, so a refund three keys sign between the two depths is not raised by its depth alone: it is still raised if a reorg then drops the skip while the refund is queued, and its mint is still mint-of-refunded-deposit. A skip the monitor took from a checkpoint’s list (initialSkipped), or one recorded before round 13, has no depth known: its refund is judged by the skip’s presence, as before, and stays watched while it is queued.

A vault and its kUSDT are a pair that no chain enforces

Section titled “A vault and its kUSDT are a pair that no chain enforces”

A burn is identified by its Kaspa outpoint and a deposit by a number its vault counts up. Neither names the other side: nothing in a burn says which vault pays it, and nothing in a deposit says which kUSDT covenant mints it. That is safe only because each vault serves one covenant from its first block to its last. If operators ever paired a new vault with a kUSDT that had lived, every earlier burn of that chain could be paid a second time from the new vault. If they paired a new kUSDT with vaults that had lived, every earlier deposit could be minted a second time. Neither can happen by an outsider’s doing, and the shipped deployer always makes vaults and covenant together.

Since round 12 the rule is written down (a vault is never replaced under a live kUSDT, nor a kUSDT over live vaults), and the operators’ checks refuse what the chains show of a broken pair: a vault that has taken fewer deposits than the minter is past, a vault that paid out before its kUSDT minted anything, a vault made after its kUSDT’s genesis, and a service state that shares only half of its pair with the deployment it is started on.

What is left:

  • Deposits made before the first mint look the same either way. A vault is public from its first block, so deposits before its kUSDT’s first mint are ordinary, and no chain can tell them from deposits an earlier covenant already minted. That case rests on the rule and on the services refusing a state made under another pairing.
  • The signers do not check the pair when they sign. The checks run in mainnet:verify, in the deployer and in the preflight (kusdt check) before a service is started, not at each signature.
  • The time of the genesis is the node’s estimate. “Made after the genesis” compares the time of the block that created the vault with the node’s time for the genesis’s DAA score, with an hour left out. A vault put under a kUSDT within that hour is not caught by this line. Until round 13 the margin was also a twentieth of the kUSDT’s age, so a vault made ten days late passed every run from day 200 on (R13-OWN-2), and the vault was dated by deployment.json’s fromBlock alone, so a file that kept the old vault’s fromBlock passed a replaced vault (R13-ONCE-2). The vault’s creation is now read from the chain: the deploy transaction in that block, or the vault’s code before and after it, and no proof fails the line on mainnet.
  • Once the minter has moved, the genesis is dated by deployment.json. While the node holds the launch’s minter (or, in mainnet:verify, its chain still shows the genesis accepted), the genesis’s DAA score is the node’s. After that the only score left is the one deployment.json records, and the line says it rests on that record. A file whose score was raised would move the genesis later and could hide a late vault, which is why each operator compares deployment.json’s SHA-256 with the others’ and runs the checks soon after the launch, when the node’s own score is still there. When the node gives no time for the score at all, the line is left out.

Blocked recipients

Section titled “Blocked recipients”

A blocked recipient is never paid. This was decided on 4 November 2026.

Ethereum USDT keeps a blacklist (isBlackListed(address)), edited by Tether. BSC-USD (0x55d398326f99059fF775485246999027B3197955) keeps none, and cannot be paused either. USDT’s blacklist stops only senders: transfer checks msg.sender, and transferFrom checks from. So the vault would still pay a blacklisted address, and the USDT would be frozen there for good. The bridge stops it before the vault:

  • Wallets ask first. Before every burn, the wallet and the web app call GET /recipients/:chain/:address and refuse an address the vault would not pay. If they cannot ask, they refuse too: a burn cannot be undone. The wallet asks again right before it sends, and neither offers the other chain for a blocked address.
  • Signers refuse. A signer will not sign a release or a refund to an address the token reports blocked. It asks every one of its RPCs, and signs nothing if they disagree. The deployment names the view to read (blocklist, per chain).
  • A burn that lands anyway is held. Its status is blocked. The coordinator checks it again every hour and pays it if the token unblocks the address. A refund to a blocked depositor waits the same way. A held record never holds up the others.

One gap: the vault can’t read a blocklist, and anyone may pay a queued payout. If the token blocks a recipient during the wait, the coordinator holds its payout, but anyone else could still pay it, and the USDT would be frozen there.

Nothing can send a held burn elsewhere. The burn commits to its address, and no key can redirect it: the vault has no re-target power. Its USDT stays in the vault as backing for as long as the address is blocked.

All this was checked on mainnet forks (npm run fork:rehearsal): a release to a blacklisted address is paid and frozen there, a blacklisted vault pays nothing, and BSC-USD keeps no blacklist. The held-then-paid flow for releases and refunds, the signers’ refusals and the vault-blocked pause were checked in npm run local:load.

A burn’s payload can be missing

Section titled “A burn’s payload can be missing”

A redemption transaction should carry the burn’s preimage in its payload. If it does not, the burn is still valid, but the bridge cannot know its chain and address from the chain alone. Anyone can give the coordinator the preimage later (POST /redeems), and it is checked against the burn owner. Until then, the burn waits.

Redemptions depend on one vault

Section titled “Redemptions depend on one vault”

kUSDT is one fungible token, whichever chain its USDT came from. A release, however, is paid by one vault:

  • A release must not exceed that vault’s backing. kUSDT minted from Ethereum deposits can be redeemed on BNB Chain only if the BNB Chain vault holds enough.
  • A single burn output, fee included, must fit in that vault’s release capacity, 250 000 USDT by default.

Wallets refuse a burn the named vault cannot pay now (above its release capacity or releaseAvailable, above its backing, or while it is paused) and offer the other chain. Since rc.18 they decide this on the vault’s own answers, read over the chain’s RPCs before the burn is built and again before it is sent (paused(), unreservedBacking(), releaseAvailable(), reservedUnits, the capacity and the fee): the coordinator’s /status is not enough on its own, and when the two disagree nothing is burnt. Before, these four came from /status alone, so a wrong or stale answer there could have a wallet burn kUSDT the vault could not pay yet. Another burn or a pause can still land between the read and the burn. If one lands anyway, it waits: the relayer retries it with its own backoff until the allowance refills, deposits on that chain bring the backing, or the owner raises the capacity within its hard bound. It never holds up other burns.

Rebalancing uses the bridge’s own paths. No key can move backing from one vault to the other. When one chain runs short, an operator holding kUSDT redeems it from the vault that has plenty, moves the USDT across (an exchange or another bridge), and deposits it into the short vault, which mints the same kUSDT back. Backing moves for 0.5 % each way plus the cost of the crossing, and no new trust is added. See Operations.

kUSDT is backed by the two vaults together. If one chain’s stablecoin depegs or is mis-minted, kUSDT minted from it can redeem real USDT from the other vault, up to the release allowances. Nothing on either chain can tell which vault a token came from, and the backing check (INV-1) counts both vaults as one. In a run on one chain, holders are paid on the other chain only while that vault holds enough. The monitor warns on /health when one chain’s releases exceed what its deposits minted by more than releaseCapacity: that is a warning, and it pauses nothing, because moving value across chains is a normal use.

USDT sent straight to a vault is lost

Section titled “USDT sent straight to a vault is lost”

Only the vault’s deposit function bridges. USDT sent to a vault’s address with a plain transfer (from an exchange withdrawal, say) names no Kaspa address and gets no deposit number: nothing is minted for it, and no function of the vault can send it back. It adds to the backing and stays there. The vault has no owner withdrawal of anything but fees, on purpose: such a power would be a way to take the backing. Always bridge through the web app or the wallet.

The daily limit can be used up by one party

Section titled “The daily limit can be used up by one party”

The signers sign for a limited amount a day, across both chains (25 000 USDT at the launch), for mints and for payouts each. Someone who uses that up keeps other people waiting until the day has passed: a deposit made then waits, and is minted when there is room again. Nothing is lost. Raising the limit only raises the money such a party needs.

Deposits: the fee rises with use. Decided on 10 November 2026. Each vault’s deposit fee goes up as the day’s deposits on it grow: 0.50 % until its deposit meter is half full, rising in a straight line to 2.50 % at a full meter, and 2.50 % while it stays full (Fees). Each vault’s meter is half the signers’ limit, so filling the limit fills both meters. At the launch values, filling both meters from empty costs 250 USDT in deposit fees on 25 000 USDT, against 125 at the flat rate, and anything deposited while a meter is full pays 2.50 %. This makes the attack cost more. It does not rule it out: a meter drains over a day, so a party willing to pay can do it again the next day. Honest depositors pay the higher rate too while a meter is more than half full, and the web app says so.

Bridging back: still the flat fee. The rising fee covers deposits only. A release’s fee is set by the vault after the kUSDT is burnt, and a burn cannot be undone, so nobody could refuse a higher fee at that point. So someone holding kUSDT can still use up the signers’ limit for payouts by bridging back that much, for the flat fee of 0.50 % (125 USDT on 25 000 at the launch). Other people’s payouts then wait, up to a day. Nothing is lost. This part is open.

Small burns are not paid

Section titled “Small burns are not paid”

A burn whose amount is at or below the release fee (5 USDT on Ethereum, 0.50 USDT on BNB Chain, or 0.50 % if higher) is not paid. It is marked unpayable, and the tokens are still burned. It is looked at again about once a minute and paid if the fee falls below its amount; nothing obliges the owner to lower the fee. A burn naming the zero address, the vault itself or the token contract’s own address is lost for good.

The wallet and the web app refuse such a burn, and warn when the fee would take more than 20 % of a redemption. Only a transaction built by hand can still lose a burn this way. Each burn output also locks 0.3 KAS for good (below).

Only plain burns are paid

Section titled “Only plain burns are paid”

Signers refuse a burn state whose borrow scheme, borrow guard or extension commitment is not zero, because a borrowable state could move to a new outpoint and be claimed twice. Such kUSDT is lost. Wallets never build one. The coordinator does not record such a state as a redemption at all, nor one with an amount of zero.

Burns lock KAS too

Section titled “Burns lock KAS too”

An ordinary kUSDT state carries the deployment’s token value, 1.5 KAS, for its storage mass, and that KAS comes back when the state is spent. A burn state can never be spent, so it carries the smaller burn value, 0.3 KAS, which is gone for good. Wallets show it as part of a redemption’s cost.

Rules the vault cannot check

Section titled “Rules the vault cannot check”

The vault cannot see Kaspa, and USDT pays a blacklisted address. Four rules rest on the signers alone:

  • a release pays the chain and address the burn owner commits to;
  • a refund pays the depositor;
  • nothing is paid to an address the token blocks;
  • a nonce is never both minted and refunded (INV-2).

A bad quorum that broke them would still be capped by the release allowance.

Forged payouts are cancelled one by one

Section titled “Forged payouts are cancelled one by one”

Three stolen signer keys can queue forged payouts of any size above the release fee, thousands of them before a guardian’s pause lands on a chain with fast blocks. The pause holds them all, and nothing leaves; but the vault cancels one payout per call (cancelRelease, cancelRefund, each an EMERGENCY operation with its 6 hour delay), and every one must be cancelled before any unpause, or it is paid to the thief afterwards (round 15, R15-OWN-1). The vault’s bytecode is not changed for this: a batch cancel is a new contract and a new audit.

  • What the operators have. Each monitor flags every queued payout an alarm names, a payout it failed closed on included, with what it pays and to whom, and keeps it on its list until it has left the queue. kusdt cancel-plan reads the whole list from the monitors’ state files and writes the announcements and the Safe’s transactions, 50 cancels to a transaction through the AccessManager’s multicall: about 1.8 M gas to schedule and 3.4 M to execute per batch. A thousand forged payouts are 20 batches each way, signed by the Safe’s threshold. A monitor takes the thousand announcements at the cost of one lookup each. Run again for payouts flagged meanwhile, it is given the earlier run’s owner-calls.json (--scheduled) and schedules only what is new, so no batch repeats a scheduled cancel and reverts (round 16, R16-OWN-1); it still reads nothing from a chain.
  • Payouts of kUSDT minted with nothing behind it (since round 16, R16-PAY-6). Their burns are real, match and are deep, so no payout alarm names them. While a mint alarm is open (mint-mismatch, mint-without-deposit, a mint of a skipped or refunded deposit), every payout still queued that was seen since the bad mint was accepted (by the monitor’s view of it, with a margin) is flagged after-unbacked-mint. While INV-1 is short they are flagged after-inv1-short from the oldest mint not yet checked, or all of them when every mint is checked, and taken off again once the backing covers what is owed. While a mint fails closed unjudged (mint-unjudged, what a mint of a nonce nobody deposited raises since rc.21), or the mints step fails closed, they are flagged after-unchecked-mint from that mint (from the oldest mint not yet checked, for the step), and taken off again once no mint that old waits to be checked and backed. This flags honest payouts too: the owners reinstate those they cancelled (reinstateRelease, a GOVERNOR call, 48 hours). A mint the monitor never read (below its checkpoint) has no time, so only INV-1 flags what it paid.
  • What is left. The cancelling work, hours to days of Safe signing for a thief who spent cents of gas on BNB Chain, overlaps the 48 hour rotation (setSigners) a theft of three keys needs anyway. A payout no monitor flagged (none of them saw it, or each was rebuilt and lost its list) is not on the plan: before the unpause the owners also read the vault’s ReleaseQueued and RefundQueued events since the theft and compare. And the flagged-payout-still-queued alarm, raised the moment a vault runs again with a flagged payout still queued, is the backstop, not a guarantee.

The monitor’s depth rule by total is a lower bound

Section titled “The monitor’s depth rule by total is a lower bound”

A signer chooses a payout’s finality tier by the payout and what it first signed within the last hour (Finality). The monitor cannot read the signers’ records. Since round 15 (R15-PAY-1) it judges by total all the same, and since round 16 by when the signers could have signed each payout: after the payout’s burn (or skip) was as deep as its own amount asks, and before the monitor first judged it queued. Of payouts of one kind on one vault that must all have been first signed within 45 minutes, one must be as deep as the tier of three fifths of their total. That catches a redemption split into small payouts by three keys. It asks for less than the signers do when payouts go to both vaults, refunds and releases alternate, or a payout was first signed long before the monitor judged it (a relay outage, a monitor that was down): such a payout is counted with no other.

Round 15 counted by the vault’s order and readyAt, and three honest events made it call an honest payout early and pause everything: a relay that failed and was tried again long after its signatures, with every signer up (R16-ST-2); a payout signed again and queued after one signed later (R16-FIX-3, R16-PAY-3); and a payoutDelay the owner lowered (R16-FIX-2, R16-PAY-4). The two cases this page named for round 15 (a signer that signed a payout early while the other two were down, a coordinator restarted long after it collected signatures) were of the same kind. None of them moves the bounds the monitor counts by now.

What the rule still rests on, each of which could make it call an honest payout early if it failed:

  • The signers’ records are whole. A signer restored from a backup holds its daily limits as spent (fuseHoldUntil, or its own hold when its journal shows a lost record), so it signs nothing new until the hold ends.
  • The network makes at least 7.5 DAA a second over the hour: the 45 minutes are measured in DAA scores.
  • A signer’s clock does not jump forward by many minutes between two signatures.
  • A burn’s place in the chain does not move after its release was signed: a reorg that deep is an incident of its own.
  • The vault’s signers are the ones the monitor knows. It counts the deployment’s five, every address it read in the vault within the window, and the new keys of a rotation announced in expectedOwnerCalls. A monitor started from an empty state within an hour after a rotation, with neither the old keys nor the announcement, counts five.

If release-before-finality or refund-before-finality names a payout whose burn (or skip) is real and was as deep as the signers’ logs say it was when they signed, the operators acknowledge the alarm as false. Nothing is lost by it.

A refund’s deposit is read by its nonce while a monitor reads the history

Section titled “A refund’s deposit is read by its nonce while a monitor reads the history”

A monitor that has no deposit cache (a new state) or one of whose RPCs starts its scan over reads the vault’s whole deposit history first. Until round 15 a refund queued in that time was never judged, and failed closed at two thirds of its wait, however honest (R15-PAY-3). Now that refund’s one deposit is read by its nonce’s topic, back from the finalized block, eight seconds a round for all such refunds together. At RPCs that answer a 5,000 block range in a few hundred milliseconds that is about a day of BNB Chain’s blocks a round, so within the refund’s wait a deposit up to some weeks old is found (much older on Ethereum, with its slower blocks). A skip’s deposit is usually days old. One the read does not reach in time still fails closed, as before. Until round 16 those eight seconds were counted from before the history’s own read of 20 seconds, so the read by nonce read nothing (R16-PAY-2); they now start with it, and once a round has found the history behind, its other refunds go straight to it. None of this was run against a real RPC.

Each mint must be judged within failClosedMs of being seen

Section titled “Each mint must be judged within failClosedMs of being seen”

Since round 16 every open mint has its own fail-closed clock from when the monitor saw it, whatever keeps it unjudged (see Monitor). That closes the ways a forged mint was kept from a verdict for good (R16-MINT-1, R16-ONCE-1, R16-PAY-1, R16-FIX-1). It also means a monitor that cannot judge mints as fast as they come pauses everything, honest or not:

  • More mints at once than its RPCs answer for in 5 minutes. With its history read to its end, a mint’s check is one read of the vault on every RPC for each of its deposits (whether it was refunded). Until rc.22 those were read one after another, about a second for a mint of three deposits at 300 ms an answer, so about 300 such mints could be judged in 5 minutes. Since round 17 the reads for the mints next in line go out eight at a time (R17-ST-2): about 2,000 mints of three deposits at 300 ms an answer are judged within failClosedMs in the tests. A monitor back from an outage (a crash, a reboot, its Kaspa node catching up) meets every mint made meanwhile at once, each seen when it is read, so that is roughly the outage a busy bridge can take without a pause; a longer one with more mints than that pauses everything. While a history is being read, each mint’s deposit is read near the head (on BNB Chain four requests of 5,000 blocks on every RPC) and, if older than two hours, by its nonce back from the finalized block, or up from a lower nonce’s deposit: a few seconds a mint, ten or more a round. A monitor rebuilt from a checkpoint hours old, while it reads a long history, can meet more mints at once than that. Take a recent checkpoint (the RUNBOOK says so); the alarm names each mint, and step 2 of “The monitor raised unverifiable for a mint” in the RUNBOOK is what an operator does.
  • An RPC that lags more than failClosedMs. A mint whose deposit its RPCs do not show yet waits on its own clock and fails closed after failClosedMs. A lag of seconds or a few minutes raises nothing, and nothing is judged short meanwhile.
  • INV-1 is later for a mint past the vault’s count. Such a mint stays out of INV-1 until its clock runs out, so that an honest mint is not called short while every RPC lags alike (R16-FIX-4). A forged mint of a nonce no one deposited is then stopped by its unverifiable at failClosedMs (5 minutes), and INV-1 a minute later; before, INV-1 stopped it after about 150 s when it was larger than the backing allowed. The pause still comes well inside a payout’s 10-minute wait; the pool’s sell cap (10 % of its KAS reserve an hour) refills by about 0.4 % of the reserve in the extra two and a half minutes. That margin is the payout wait’s: its kUSDT can be burnt, released and queued within a minute or so of the mint, so with a wait the owner lowered (it may go down to 5 minutes) a pause at failClosedMs could come seconds before the payout is paid (R17-PAY-2). failClosedMs must stay at most half the wait, with a round, the pauses and a mined block (2.5 minutes) inside it too: the preflight check says so, a mainnet monitor refuses to start on an announced setPayoutDelay it does not fit, and a running one says so on /health when a vault’s wait no longer fits. Lower failClosedMs first (RUNBOOK, “Changing the payout wait”).
  • An RPC that reports a deposit under another transaction than before is read again once a day at most, and a second time within a day the chain fails closed after failClosedMs until it is taken out of evmRpcs. That record is kept in memory, so a restart gives such an RPC its one reading again.
  • Time away. After more than 90 seconds without a round (a host that was off), each open mint’s clock moves on by the time away, so the time it went unjudged while the monitor ran still counts. Until rc.22 it started again at the start, and a monitor that kept restarting, a few minutes up and more than 90 s away each time, never counted a forged mint past the vault’s count, on its clock or in INV-1 (R17-DIFF-1). The time from the monitor’s last finished round to its start counts as away, so one that dies inside long rounds and comes back each time delays the alarm by the time it spends that way; one that finishes no round at all is a crash-loop.
  • A reorg that drops a mint and accepts it again. The mint comes back with the record it had: its first sighting, its clock and its verdict (R17-MINT-1). Until rc.22 it came back as a mint just seen, so a miner who could reorg it every four minutes kept a forged mint from any alarm. The records of dropped mints are kept for a day, 1,024 at most; more than that at once is no honest network, and the monitor fails closed (mints-dropped-full) rather than forget one.

None of this was run against a real RPC; the tests use stand-in RPCs on a faked clock.

A governance action credits the mint allowance only up to the age its signers signed

Section titled “A governance action credits the mint allowance only up to the age its signers signed”

The mint allowance refills with the age of the minter’s own UTXO, and every spend of the minter starts that age again. A mint signs the age it claims, so nobody can change it. Since rc.16 a governance action (an unpause, a change of limits or signers, a skip, a recovery proposal or veto) signs its age too. Before rc.16 it did not, and anyone who saw a governance transaction before it was accepted could send a copy that claimed a smaller age and threw the built-up allowance away. That is closed: a copy that claims another age fails the signature check.

What remains is smaller:

  • The time after the signed age is not credited. The signers sign an age a little below the UTXO’s age when they sign (the tools leave a margin of 600 DAA, one minute), and the action lands later. The allowance that refills in between, minutes to hours of it, is not credited. When the signed age already covers a full refill, nothing is left out.
  • An action signed for age 0 credits no refill. A rotation of the signers, a recovery proposal and a veto are signed for age 0 unless the signers say otherwise, and any other action can be: a signed age is also a wait, because a transaction is valid only once the minter UTXO is that old, and a spend of the minter between signing and sending makes a new UTXO at age zero. After an action at age 0, a large deposit can wait for the refill, up to a day for a full allowance.
  • An action signed for an age above 0 can be delayed. Each spend of the minter starts the UTXO’s age again, and while spends keep landing closer together than the age signed, the action cannot be sent. It is not voided. The spends can come from outside the team: every deposit leads to a mint and anyone can make a deposit (it costs a deposit each time), and whoever holds three signer keys can mint one unit at a time, or propose and veto a recovery in turn. That is why the actions that answer a theft of keys default to age 0, which nothing can delay. For the others (an unpause, new limits, a skip, the minter’s deposit) the signers pass --age 0 when mints are flowing. Holding mints on the coordinator stops only the coordinator’s own new mints.
  • A guardian pause credits nothing for the time before it, and a recovery carried out credits nothing for its 72-hour wait. Those two steps claim no age at all: a pause must never wait on a clock, and a recovery has no signers to sign an age.

In each case mints wait until the allowance has refilled. Nothing is minted, moved or lost.

The coordinator keeps everything in memory

Section titled “The coordinator keeps everything in memory”

The title is the old one, and no longer the whole truth (corrected in round 12). Since rc.16 the coordinator moves closed records out of its state file into an archive folder beside it (<statePath>.archive), which is on disk only: a redemption that was paid or can never be paid, and a deposit that was minted or refunded, once no reorg the journals cover could bring it back. And its index of kUSDT outputs for wallets holds at most 200,000 outputs: past that the smallest leave the index and are not listed by GET /tokens, though they stay spendable by a wallet that knows their state (/health says when the index is full). Mints and payouts never read that index.

What still lives in memory, and grows with use:

  • Open records. A deposit not yet minted or refunded, a burn not yet paid, a refund record, and up to 2,000 burns too small to pay their own fee (past that the smallest are archived). Each open burn costs whoever makes it the KAS under it, and they are paid and closed as fast as the release allowance lets them.
  • The index of 200,000 outputs, about 170 MB of the 768 MB the coordinator is given.
  • The index of 50,000 liquidity positions (since round 15), about 37 MB, in a file of its own beside the state file. Anyone can make positions: every pool add makes one, of a single share if its sender likes, with about 0.2 KAS under it that remove gives back. Unbounded and in the state file, some 500,000 of them took the coordinator’s heap. Past 50,000 those with the fewest shares leave the index and are not listed by GET /positions, which then says truncated with the shares at or below which positions may be missing, and /health warns. They stay their holder’s to redeem with a wallet that knows them; pushing a real position out takes 50,000 positions of more shares each. Mints, payouts and the pool’s own following never read the index.
  • A look-up in the archive reads the last 8 MB of one file. A record older than that in its file is on the disk and no longer found by the API: by the project’s own arithmetic that takes about two million redemptions. Nobody outside can aim records at one file: which file a record lands in depends on a random salt in the folder’s meta.json. The API process has to read that salt to look records up, though (since round 14 it reads it again whenever meta.json changes, so it follows a folder the coordinator set aside and started again). Whoever takes over the API process can therefore choose burns, senders or Kaspa owners whose records all land in one file (some 256 tries each, offline), and with some ten thousand such burns or deposits, each paid for on chain and closed, push the older records of that file out of the 8 MB a look-up reads. Those records are then answered as not found (pending or 404) and a sender’s or owner’s archived list says it is cut; a paid burn reported again is looked up in the vault once more, found paid, and nothing is paid twice. Nothing is minted, paid or stalled. What an operator does after such a break-in, with the coordinator stopped: rebuild the API process’s machine or container as after any break-in, and to give the salt’s protection back, move the archive folder aside; the coordinator starts a new one with a new salt, and the old folder’s records stay on the disk, found by hand (grep for the burn’s transaction id or the sender in its files) until it is put back.

The earlier figure (about 250,000 burns of one base unit exhaust its memory) was the state before the archive. With it the figures above are the code’s own bounds; none was measured under load on a running coordinator. No funds are at risk either way: the coordinator holds no key that moves them. Since rc.16 it no longer records burns that could never be paid (empty ones, and states with borrowing or an extension).

The monitor has a smaller form of the same limit: it keeps a record of every real burn for good. A record of an output that only looked like a burn is dropped once that output is spent.

A signer keeps its book of burns (at most 20,000 records, 6 to 8 MB) inside its state file, and that file is written whole. Until round 12 its follower wrote it every two seconds, changed or not. Now the follower writes it when what it read changed something (a burn, a move of the minter, a reorg) and otherwise every five minutes. A signature still writes the whole file before it leaves the signer, and so does every pass that read a burn: with a full book that is megabytes a write. The book stays in that file on purpose: the records and the follower’s position must be on the disk as of one moment, which one file gives and two would have to be made to give.

An address that holds too many coins cannot be listed

Section titled “An address that holds too many coins cannot be listed”

A Kaspa node (kaspad 2.1.0) answers the coins at an address all at once or not at all. It has no call that lists them a page at a time, and none that answers about one output. Anyone can send an address coins. The services take at most 100 MB of such an answer, about 246,000 coins, and refuse a longer one before any of it is in memory. So an address that holds more can no longer be read by them, for as long as the coins stay there. Filling one costs whoever does it the coins for good: by the project’s own arithmetic at least 0.1 KAS a coin, some 25,000 KAS, and hours of the network’s block space. That was not measured on a network.

Since round 11 this no longer stops a payout or pauses the bridge:

  • A burn at such an address is still paid. The address of a burn is fixed by its amount, chain and recipient, and nothing can ever be spent from it, so coins sent there stay for good. Each signer and the coordinator also keep the burns their own followers read from the transactions the chain accepted, with the block that accepted each, and take a burn from that record when its address cannot be listed. A signer still signs only for a burn that is final on its own node, and checks on the spot that the block is still on its node’s chain.
  • The minter and the pool are still followed. A monitor that cannot list the minter’s or the pool’s own address says so on /health, with the address, and pauses nothing. It follows both through every accepted transaction as before.
  • The coordinator’s API no longer reads the pool’s address (since rc.18). Round 12 found that it still did, for every GET /pool, in the line its mint and release loops’ reads wait in: with the pool’s address filled, one request a second kept that line growing, and nothing was minted or released until an operator stepped in. /pool now answers the pool output’s age from what the coordinator’s follower saw accepted, which it keeps in its state, and everything else the API asks the node goes over connections of its own.

The launch check finds the genesis there too (round 12). mainnet:verify used to list the whole address of the initial minter and of the initial pool, and about 158,000 coins sent to either made it fail for every operator, at the launch and in every later run. It now asks for the one output, and where the address cannot be listed it proves the output from the transactions the chain accepted since the launch’s scanFrom: accepted with the expected script, covenant ID and value, and not spent by any accepted transaction up to the tip.

Execute finishes the launch there too (round 13, R13-OWN-1). The genesis minter’s address follows from plan.json alone, so it is public as soon as the plan goes to the operators and reviewers, days before the launch, and anyone can fill it first. mainnet:execute still listed each step’s output address whole, and with the minter’s address filled it never got past the genesis: every rerun waited for the same genesis and failed the same way. It now reads through the services’ own node client and, where an address cannot be listed, proves the step’s output from the transactions the chain accepted since a chain block it recorded before the send, as verify does. A deployer address filled past what can be read is the one case it cannot get past alone: it then sends nothing and asks the operator to name fresh coins (--kaspa-funding), each proven from the chain before it is spent.

What is left, until a kaspad release has a paged read:

  • Execute’s proof needs the chain since the step’s recorded block. A node keeps about 30 hours. A stopped execute rerun later than that, at a filled address, cannot prove its step: it says so and sends nothing new, and the way on is a node that still keeps the block (an archival one). None of it was run against a node holding that many coins.

  • Verify’s proof needs the chain since scanFrom. A node keeps about 30 hours. An operator who runs the launch check later than that on a filled address gets a line that says the output could not be looked for: a FAIL at the launch, a warning in a later run (--launched), never a pass. The read takes about a minute per hour of chain and is given 15 minutes unless told otherwise; none of it was run against a node holding that many coins.

  • A coordinator started from a checkpoint asks its node once for the minter’s DAA score, by listing the minter’s address. At a filled address it cannot, and it uses the checkpoint’s own score, within what a full refill needs. With a full allowance, as at genesis, that is no age at all and the mint goes through. A checkpoint whose score is too low, or that has none, at an address filled past 246,000 coins, gives a coordinator no age to count (or too much) until the minter moves, and a batch larger than the allowance left is refused for that long. Until rc.18 the monitor’s checkpoint, the usual one to rebuild a coordinator from, had no score at all, so this took no wrong file, only a filled address at a rebuild (round 13, R13-MINT-2). Since round 13 the monitor keeps the score the coordinator keeps (the DAA score of the block that accepted the minter’s move), so both services’ checkpoints carry it; a coordinator that starts with no score and cannot read the address says so once in its log, and kusdt check warns. What is left: a checkpoint taken from a monitor of rc.18 or older, or edited by hand, while the minter has not moved since. The operator copies initialMinter.utxo.blockDaaScore from the checkpoint of a monitor of this version that followed the minter’s last move itself.

  • A signer that never read the burn’s block has only the UTXO set. That is a signer started from a checkpoint taken after the burn, one that went on from a checkpoint after more than about 30 hours away, or one whose book of 20,000 records pushed the burn out. For a burn at a filled address it refuses, and never guesses. Three signers that did read the block are enough. Operators keep it that way by restoring a signer from its state backup (its records come with it) instead of starting it from a fresh checkpoint while burns wait unpaid. Pushing a burn out is not one signer’s bad luck: every follower reads the same transactions and trims its book by the same rule, so an outsider who pushes a burn out of one book pushes it out of all of them at once (round 15, R15-ST-1). Up to rc.19 any P2SH-owned kUSDT output counted, and 21,001 outputs of the outsider’s own script, of the burn’s amount, spent back afterwards, were enough. Since round 15 a record whose transaction’s payload names it as a burn (its owner is the burn owner of the payload’s chain and address) goes after every other record, and the coordinator also keeps any record a waiting redemption names. Pushing such a burn out takes 20,000 larger real burns, kUSDT destroyed for good, held unretired at the same time. What is left: a burn with no payload (a wallet always writes one; a hand-made transaction may not) is protected in the coordinator’s book once its owner gives the preimage, but not in the signers’ books, which learn the chain and address only when asked to sign. Such a burn at a filled address can still be pushed out of every signer’s book by outputs of the burner’s choosing, and then waits for good. Once a signer has signed a burn’s release, its book keeps that burn with the named ones until the payout is paid (round 17, R17-PAY-1; up to rc.22 a release signed over a week before went first, paid or not).

  • A monitor that has no record of the burn fails closed, and an outsider can make it. A monitor looks for a burn it has no record of in the UTXO set when the release is queued. If that address cannot be read for two minutes it raises unverifiable and everything is paused: with three stolen keys an attacker chooses the address a forged burn is looked for at, so “cannot read” must not pass. It takes no keys to set this off, though. Equal burns (one amount, chain and recipient) share one address, so one filled address serves any number of burns. Anyone can fill it once, make small burns of exactly that state with no payload now and then (they are real burns, the signers keep them in their books, and nothing pays them until someone asks), and later ask for one to be paid. The signers sign, as they should. A monitor without that burn’s record then pauses the bridge: both vaults for at least their unpause delay, the minter and the pool until 4 of 5 unpause them. One planted burn, one pause, for a release fee each, until that monitor has the records.

    Since round 12 a monitor gets them. A monitor’s checkpoint, asked with its operator bearer, lists every burn it knows that no vault has paid (initialBurns), and a monitor started from that checkpoint, or going on from it after a gap, takes the list in and judges a release against it by owner, amount and depth without needing the address. It still asks the UTXO set while the release waits: a node that answers “not there” ends the record, and the release is release-without-burn. What is left:

    • a monitor that already runs from an older checkpoint has no list. A list is taken only with its own checkpoint: the operators rebuild that monitor from a fresh one, or restore its own state backup (the runbook);
    • a checkpoint lists at most 20,000 burns, the largest first, and says how many it left out (burnsLeftOut). Whoever holds that many larger unpaid P2SH-owned kUSDT outputs at one time pushes the small ones out of the list. A monitor’s own state backup leaves nothing out;
    • when every monitor was rebuilt after a burn was planted, no monitor has its record. The operators check such a burn by hand and add it to the list themselves (the runbook);
    • the list is the operators’ word, like the list of skips: an entry that is not a real burn would let a forged release for it pass that monitor while the address cannot be read. It comes from a monitor the operators run.

  • A monitor that went on from a checkpoint after a long gap forgets the burns it had read last. A reorg just below its old position went by unread, so it keeps only the burns that were already deeper than the deepest finality tier (an hour) when it stopped, and asks the UTXO set about those too while a release waits. The rest come back with the checkpoint’s list. The signers and the coordinator forget all of theirs in that case.

  • A refund is not signed while the minter rests at a filled address. A signer proves its view of the minter by finding it in the UTXO set. The minter moves to a new address with any mint or governance action, and refunds are signed again.

  • A service’s own address. The coordinator’s funding address and a monitor’s guardian address are read whole once (the services keep their own coins from then on), and again when the coins kept are in doubt or run out. Past about 246,000 coins no read takes the address, and nothing in the services or in a wallet can list those coins, so nobody can compound them: below that, the operator can still compound them into a few with the key. A monitor whose guardian address is filled before its first read has only the coins it sees paid to the key after its cursor, and its operator funds it with a new payment (the coordinator’s case is in the runbook, “can’t be read, so nothing is minted”). Until rc.18 a monitor whose list of guardian coins was in doubt paid a pause only from a read of the address, and any pause the node refused for a missing input put the list in doubt, also when the missing input was the pool or minter coin, which anyone can make happen by moving the pool. At a filled guardian address that stopped every Kaspa pause of the minter and the pool, on every monitor, for good (round 13, R13-SURF-1). Since round 13 a read that fails falls back to the coins kept, a refusal whose state coin has moved on puts nothing in doubt, and one whose state coin had not moved takes out only the guardian coins that pause spent. Since rc.22 not even all of those: only a coin known to be gone (a block the monitor fetched spends it, or its follower read the spend) leaves the list, and the others are set aside until the address is read or ten minutes pass, so two monitors on one guardian key whose pauses race no longer forget each other’s honest coins. What is left: a monitor with no coins kept (none was ever paid to the key after its last good read) and a filled address sends no Kaspa pause until its operator sends the guardian a coin. /health says the address could not be read once a read failed; nothing reads it to find out sooner, as that read is the one the record exists to avoid. The operator’s way out for good is a fresh guardian key (deploy/RUNBOOK.md, “A filled guardian address”). The pool keeper’s address is kept the same way since round 13. Until then the keeper read it for every tick, and once anyone had filled it every tick stopped while its /health answered 200 (R13-SURF-3); since then its /health answers 503 with the reason when a due tick cannot go out for a minute, or its rounds have failed for that long. Its operator can also give it a new key.

  • What a wallet is shown, and what it can spend. kUSDT Wallet and the web app run in a browser, which takes a node’s answer whole: they read at most 64 MB of one, about 158,000 coins (some 16,000 KAS to fill an address, by the same arithmetic), and on a slow line a list times out well before that. That is below the services’ 100 MB. A kUSDT state’s address follows from its owner and its amount, so an outsider can fill the address of a state a holder has, or send a holder a dust state at an address filled beforehand. Until rc.18 one such address took all of that owner’s kUSDT out of kUSDT Wallet and out of the web app with KasWare: they asked the node for all of the owner’s state addresses in one call, it failed, and with it the balance and every send, redemption and sale. No shipped wallet could spend a state its node could not list. Since rc.18:

    • The rest of the holder’s kUSDT is not affected. When the one read fails for its length or for time, each address is read on its own. An address that could not be listed is not asked about again for ten minutes (one minute after a timeout), one look reads at most 64 addresses on their own, and at most 64 such addresses are remembered: whoever fills addresses does not decide how much the wallet reads.
    • The kUSDT at the filled address is shown apart, as not checked, and is not in the balance. In kUSDT Wallet’s Send a box spends it: the transaction is built on the wallet’s own record of the state (what its node showed it before the address was filled, or what its own transaction made) or, where it has none, on the coordinator’s. A wrong record costs nothing: every output is still built by the wallet from what you approve, and a Kaspa signature commits to the spent output’s script and amount, so the node refuses the transaction unless the state is there exactly as recorded. What is kept from such a send goes to a state of another amount, so to another address. The wallet never chooses such a state by itself, never builds the pool on an unchecked state, and never offers a state the node says is absent.
    • The wallet keeps its own record of its states and looks for them on the node beside the coordinator’s list. The web app with KasWare keeps the same record in the page’s storage (public outpoints and amounts only, since rc.19), so it outlives a reload; with the page’s storage blocked it lasts until the page is closed. Since rc.19 what the wallet’s own transaction spends stays in the record, marked, until that transaction is seen accepted (an hour at most), so the input of a pool transaction that lost the race is still there; change that waits in the mempool is kept while its transaction waits; and looks less than a minute apart count as one, so a site asking for balances often does not age the record.
    • The coordinator answers past its own 100 MB too (since rc.19). Above 100 MB at one address it cannot read the address even coin by coin. Until rc.19 GET /tokens then failed for that owner, for good, since nothing removes the coins, and a wallet with no record of its own (a new install, another device, the web app with KasWare after any reload) showed none of that key’s kUSDT and could send none of it. Now the coordinator reads the owner’s other addresses as before, answers their states, and lists its own records of the states at the full address apart (unread: outpoint, state, the KAS the coin carries). It does not ask the node about such an address again for ten minutes. A wallet treats those records as states its node cannot show: apart, not in the balance, and in kUSDT Wallet spent only with Send’s box ticked, when the node judges them.
    • What became of the wallet’s own pool transaction is asked by its id (since round 15, R15-CL-4). Until then kUSDT Wallet listed the mempool at the addresses the transaction pays, its own among them, which anyone can fill with transactions no block takes: every look was “not known”, a swap that lost the race for the pool was reported as sent and not tried again, and what it held stayed out of use for ten minutes. A mempool read that fails no longer stops a look; a lost race is still believed only on two looks in a row. The UTXO set is still read at those addresses: an address filled with real coins (which costs their KAS, paid to the holder) still makes the outcome “sent, not confirmed”, and the ten minute mark frees what it held. What is left:
    • kUSDT received while the coordinator cannot answer at all is not shown (its node down, or its budget used up), because the wallet learns of new states from the coordinator. It is not lost: it shows once the coordinator answers again. A full address no longer stops it from answering.
    • The web app with KasWare shows and uses the rest and has no way to spend the unchecked state. kUSDT Wallet with the same recovery phrase does (both derive the same Kaspa key).
    • A sale or a liquidity deposit never takes an unchecked state. Send a part of it to your own address first: both new states then sit at other addresses.
    • A liquidity position at a filled address is listed as not checked, from its record, and can be removed: the node decides, as for a send.
    • The pool at a filled address is not traded with by any wallet: they refuse a pool they could not check, and say why, and a provider cannot withdraw through one either. It ends when a transaction changes the pool’s state (its address follows from the state alone), which a wallet’s swap cannot do there. The keeper’s tick does it while the keeper is armed and more than half of the cap is in use: each tick then lowers the count. Since round 13 the keeper reads no address for that; until then it read the pool’s address in every round while armed, and past about 246,000 coins it sent nothing while its /health answered 200 (R13-ST-1). Otherwise an operator moves it by hand (runbook, “An address holds too many coins”): a tick while more than half of the cap is in use, else a small buy of an amount nobody can guess, built from the coordinator’s view of the pool without reading the address. A tick is no way off a pool whose breaker has settled (nothing in use, its reference at its reserve): that pool comes out of a tick unchanged, at the same address, and whoever filled that one address holds the pool there until someone sends the buy. A paused pool takes neither: only its admin moves it (an unpause, or a key change to a new admin key). Until an operator acts, every swap, deposit and withdrawal waits. No funds are at risk, and mints and payouts are not affected.

  • The pool’s age, on a coordinator that did not see the pool’s output accepted. That is a coordinator on its first start after an upgrade from before rc.18, one started from a checkpoint, or one after a reorg that undid a pool move, and only until the pool next moves. It learns the age by one read of the pool’s address. If that address holds more coins than can be read, /pool answers poolDaa: null, which claims no age: a quote made from it counts no refill of the breaker’s room, and while more than half of the cap is in use a pool transaction built from that answer alone cannot be sent (readyAtDaa is null). Any accepted pool transaction that changes the pool’s state moves it to a new address and ends it (above: not a wallet’s swap there; the keeper’s tick, or one built by hand with the age the keeper’s follower holds or an explorer shows). No funds are at risk, and mints and payouts are not affected. This case was tested against a stand-in node only.

  • A burn without a payload, on a coordinator that did not read its block, is confirmed only from the UTXO set. At a filled address its claim comes to nothing there. Wallets always attach the payload.

A flood can take the public answers down, and the machine’s network with them

Section titled “A flood can take the public answers down, and the machine’s network with them”

Until round 12 the coordinator’s API and the monitor’s status were answered by the same process, on the same thread, as the loops that mint and pay and the rounds that judge and pause. A flood from very many addresses at once cost those processes the handling of every request, whatever was counted per address, and a monitor that could not finish its rounds for five minutes failed closed and paused everything.

Since the work after round 12 they are answered by processes of their own, each in its own container: kusdt api from the files the coordinator writes, kusdt status from the files the monitor writes, both through read-only mounts. The coordinator listens for nobody outside its own container and fetches the burn claims from the API process itself, at a rate it sets; the monitor’s own listener takes the operator bearer for every request. A flood of requests now costs the API process or the status process, and at worst takes the public answers down. It costs the coordinator and the monitor nothing: no request is handled, parsed or refused by them. What is left:

  • The machine’s network. The processes share a host, its network card and its bandwidth. A flood large enough to fill the link to the machine also slows what the coordinator sends to the signers and the chains, and what the monitor reads, as it would for any service on that machine. That is the hosting provider’s protection to give (a provider that absorbs floods, or a CDN in front of the public names), not the software’s. The mainnet packages put the coordinator and the monitor each on a machine of its own with their public process; the monitor’s public name can sit behind a CDN without changing anything for integrators.
  • The machine’s CPU and memory, with the shipped limits. Each public process has a CPU and memory cap of its own in the compose files, below the machine’s, so it cannot take the money processes’ share; an operator who removes the caps removes that. Until round 15 the coordinator’s package gave its containers 16.25 GB in all on a 16 GB machine (R15-PUB-4), so a flood could still reach the node through the host’s memory. Now they add up to 14.75 GB (the node 12 GB, the coordinator 1 GB, the API process 1.5 GB, Caddy 256 MB), and the API process’s share is sized for what it holds at its bounds: the token index and the positions twice while it reads one again, the owners’ answers and two node answers. A node given more (KASPAD_MEM) on the same machine overcommits it again; the 12 GB for the node is not yet measured on mainnet.
  • What a wallet is told while the coordinator is away. The API process says so rather than answer from an old copy: past 15 seconds without a new copy /health is 503 and /pool refuses with retryAt, and a burn claim is refused at once (a burn is still found on the chain). The other answers are as of the coordinator’s last save, which was always a few seconds behind what it knew. A fresh copy of a coordinator that is not following is judged by the followers’ own ages, which the copy carries since round 15: /pool refuses once the Kaspa follower has not seen its node move on for 60 seconds or lags it by more than 600 DAA, and /health is 503 for that and for a chain not read in full for five minutes. Since round 16 a scan counts as moving on only while the node calls itself synced and keeps a tenth of the network’s pace, and a chain whose reads go through is still said to be stuck once its lowest head across the RPCs has not risen for five minutes or one RPC has trailed the best by over two minutes for five. The node’s own word on being synced allows a sink up to about ten minutes old (kaspad’s rule): a node catching up from less behind than that, with its peers, is counted as current, and /pool can then be that far behind until it catches up (a swap built on it fails at the node, nothing more). Otherwise the pool state a wallet is handed can be up to about a minute behind the chain at the worst (the 15 seconds of the copy and the 60 of the follower do not add up: the follower’s age already counts the copy’s); a swap built on a state that moved meanwhile fails at the node, as one did before. The records (/status, /deposits, /redeems) are not refused on these grounds: they say what the coordinator knows, which is what a stalled coordinator acts on.
  • The public answers’ own limits, unchanged. Each address gets 240 requests a minute, owners’ reads and the archive’s are held to budgets of time, and a wallet can be shown a list of its outputs that is up to a minute old (marked stale), or be told to ask again (retryAt). These now protect the API process’s answers, not the money.
  • The node the API process asks. The API process reads the coordinator’s Kaspa node over connections of its own, in a line of its own, so a request never makes the coordinator’s reads wait. A read of an owner’s address still has the node build the whole list of coins there (kaspad 2.1.0 has nothing narrower). The API process holds four connections to the node at the most, whatever it is asked, and the shipped node takes 128 clients (--rpcmaxclients, since round 15; it was 16). The node is shared, and no bound of the code holds a process someone took over: from the API container it can open connections and ask the node for work up to what the node itself bears (in kaspad 2.1.0’s source --rpcmaxclients counts gRPC clients only, and nothing caps the wRPC JSON clients the services are: read in the source, not run). That is more than a slowdown: a node kept busy, or out of connections or file handles, can make the coordinator’s own reads time out and its connections fail to come back, and mints, payouts and burn confirmations then stop for as long as the container is held. No money is at risk. The coordinator’s /health says it within a minute (the coordinator's Kaspa follower last saw its node move on N s ago). What the operator does: docker compose stop api, which closes every connection the API process holds; the coordinator’s connections come back by themselves; then find how it was taken over before it is started again from the image (docker compose up -d --force-recreate api). An operator who wants that closed for good gives the API process a node of its own.

The monitor’s public checkpoint lists every deposit Kaspa skipped, and nothing caps that list. The monitor builds it for its file at most every ten seconds, and no sooner than twenty times what building it took, up to a minute, so a checkpoint can be that old. A checkpoint is a place to start from, and one a minute old is as good.

Pool contention

Section titled “Pool contention”

Each swap spends one exact pool state. When several users swap at once, all but one must re-quote and re-sign. This is the price of being immune to sandwich attacks. The wallet goes ahead on its own only when the new quote is at least as good as the one you confirmed; a worse one sends nothing and asks you again (kUSDT Wallet in its own window; with KasWare, the web app on its Swap page, since rc.18).

A node that takes a pool transaction has not accepted it: of two transactions spending the same pool state, each taken by a different node, only one is ever accepted. The wallet therefore waits (up to 30 seconds) to see which it was. It says “Swapped”, “Added” or “Removed” only once its node shows the transaction accepted. If another transaction won, it frees the kUSDT and coins the lost one had set aside at once and goes again on the new pool state under the rule above. If neither is shown in time, it says “sent, not confirmed yet”, keeps what the transaction spends set aside, and looks again at every refresh; Activity then says “did not go through” or drops the mark. What is left: the wallet learns this from one node. A node that is behind, or lies, can delay the answer or give a wrong one. A wrong “lost” cannot make a swap happen twice (the next try must spend one of the same coins or the same kUSDT state, so it is void if the first was accepted), but the wallet’s words about the first try are then wrong until the next refresh shows the coins gone.

The pool caps sells

Section titled “The pool caps sells”

The net KAS sells take out of the pool, less what buys bring in, may be at most 10 % of its KAS reserve per hour (the circuit breaker). It bounds a dump of forged kUSDT, but it also slows a large honest sell, which must wait for buys or the next window. Buys are not limited. Because it counts the net flow, round trips (a buy, then a sell back) cannot use up the room; until 5 November 2026 they could.

Pool transactions wait a few seconds while more than half of the cap is in use

Section titled “Pool transactions wait a few seconds while more than half of the cap is in use”

The sell cap refills with the age of the pool’s own UTXO, and time a pool transaction does not claim is lost. So while sells have used more than half of the cap, every pool transaction (a swap, a deposit, a withdrawal) must claim at least 50 DAA of that age, about 5 seconds: the pool can’t be spent more often than that, and each spend refills at least that much. The wallet and the web app wait for it on their own and send at that moment, and a transaction right after another pool transaction can take a few seconds longer. At or below half of the cap there is no wait, and a withdrawal while the pool is paused never waits (the cap’s clock stands still during a pause). Withdrawals are never refused for it.

Each pool state then makes one slot every 5 seconds, which goes to whichever transaction the network takes first. A bot that always gets there first can keep every other pool transaction out while more than half of the cap is in use: for at most half an hour after a full cap was sold (an hour and a half if half the pool was also withdrawn), unless it also keeps selling about a whole cap an hour. A seller who wins one race in two waits a slot or two.

A bot can also let the pool’s UTXO age and then spend it claiming only those 5 seconds, so the cap refills slowly. The operators run a keeper against this: once it sees such a spend, it settles the cap’s clock in each slot for the next hour, 2.5 seconds after the wallets would, so a bot that lands late no longer lands at all. One that lands within those 2.5 seconds wastes that much of each slot, and one that stays under the keeper’s 5 second threshold is never caught and can halve the refill while nobody else trades. Either way no KAS leaves. Anyone may run a keeper.

A pool so small that its cap is under 720 sompi (under 0.0001 KAS of KAS reserve) refills nothing on the shortest claim, because the refill rounds down. The pool’s first deposit takes its admin’s signature, and so does the first deposit after every provider has left (which starts the cap again at the new reserve), so nobody can open it with dust. One case is left: if the last provider leaves while someone keeps a dust position in the pool, it is not empty, anyone may add to it, and its cap stays the dust’s; a bot spending every slot can then keep it from growing, and a pause and a fresh start by the admin are the way out.

The admin’s deposit into a pool that every provider has left is signed for one pool state, and the admin key is kept offline. While that pool is open anyone can move it to a new state for a small fee (a tick every 5 seconds, or a dust swap), and a deposit carried over from the offline computer then no longer fits. No money is at risk, and it costs whoever does it a fee each time. The operators’ way through: the guardian pauses the pool, which nobody but the admin can then move, and the admin sends the unpause and the deposit together. A pool that was never opened can’t be moved this way at all: until its first deposit only the admin and the guardian can spend it.

That way through works only while at most half of the pool’s cap is in use, and an emptied pool usually starts above half (found in round 12; this page and the runbook said otherwise). An emptied pool holds about 1,000 sompi, so its cap is 100 sompi. The last withdrawal after net selling leaves the count at up to 200, and anyone can put it above half with a sell of 51 sompi. Above half every entry must claim 50 DAA, the admin’s deposit included, so the deposit cannot follow the unpause at once; a pause does not lower the count; and the keeper’s ticks do not either, because a cap of 100 sompi refills one sompi per 360 DAA of claimed age and each tick claims about 70. Two things bring the count to half: one tick on a pool output that nobody has spent for 90 minutes, or a dust buy that pays in the difference and has the guardian’s pause right behind it. Against someone who spends the pool in every 5-second slot the first is impossible and the second has to win one slot from them. Until it does, that pool cannot be reopened, for as long as they pay about 42 KAS an hour in fees. No money is in the pool then, and the way out that always works is a new pool. The steps are in the operators’ runbook (“The pool admin’s deposit won’t land”). The next pool covenant is to let the admin’s first deposit into an emptied pool claim no age.

A spend of the pool or the minter that no block can take

Section titled “A spend of the pool or the minter that no block can take”

Closed by the covenants since rc.22. An outside review of rc.19 (12 November 2026) ran this on the Kaspa node version mainnet uses: a node that builds blocks keeps such a transaction too, and refuses the pause, the mint or the trade that would replace it, so the extra Kaspa nodes of earlier candidates did not end it, and neither did sending a transaction straight to a node that builds blocks. rc.22 changes the pool’s and the minter’s covenants so that a node accepts only spends of them that a block can take (below). The remedies of rc.21 and before, described after that, stay as a second line.

A Kaspa node accepts a transaction that spends an output still waiting in its mempool without checking the age that transaction claims for it. A block does check. So a transaction can be made that ordinary nodes keep for up to 24 hours and that no block ever includes. If it spends the pool’s coin, those nodes refuse every other pool transaction as a double spend for as long as they keep it. Anyone can do this to the pool, at any time, for the fee of one plain transaction, and again when it expires. The same can be done to the minter’s coin by anyone who has seen a signed mint or governance action before a block took it: the signers sign what is minted or changed, not the transaction that carries it, so the same signatures fit into a transaction of anyone’s making with a coin of their own. Every mint the coordinator sends is seen that way for the second or so before it is in a block. And it can be done by anyone at all while a recovery proposal stands. (Until round 12 this page named only the coordinator and a holder of three signer keys. That was too few.)

What it costs while it stands: swaps, deposits and withdrawals of liquidity sent through ordinary nodes are refused or stay unconfirmed, mints wait, and a pause of the pool or the minter, or a veto, sent through an ordinary node does not land. Nothing is taken by it, the vaults’ pauses on Ethereum and BNB Chain do not depend on it, and it ends as soon as any real spend of that coin reaches a block.

Since rc.22 the covenants refuse such transactions. Every entry of the pool and of the minter checks every input of its transaction (require_settled_inputs in bridge_mint.ag). The pool’s or the minter’s own coin asks exactly the age its entry claims, and claims an age above 0 only once that coin is in a block. Every other input asks no age, is a coin already in a block or another output of the transaction that made the pool’s or the minter’s coin, and is a plain Schnorr P2PK coin or a coin of the pool’s or kUSDT’s own covenants. A transaction has at most 8 inputs. A script can tell a coin in a block from one only in the node’s mempool (the node gives the latter the DAA score u64::MAX, which reads as -1), so a pool or minter transaction a node accepts is one a block can take. This also closes a second way to park a spend, found in the design review of 12 November 2026, that the covenants before rc.22 and the checks below could not see: a funding coin whose own script passes only while its parent is unconfirmed (a P2SH script that reads its own DAA score), with no age claimed anywhere. The engine tests in bridge/kaspa/tests/acceptance_parity.rs build every pool and minter entry with these shapes and seeded random ones, as a node’s mempool sees them and as a block does. The checks of waiting transactions described below stay, as a second line and for deployments made before rc.22.

What that costs:

  • Every pool transaction shows the pool’s whole program, which is about 7.8 KB larger; a minter transaction is about 2.8 KB larger. Their fees rise by about a third.
  • At most 8 inputs: a buy leaves room for 6 funding coins, a sale, a deposit or a withdrawal for 5. A wallet with many small coins merges them first, also when the coins that fit would leave change too small for the network to keep (round 17, R17-CL-1). Change too small to keep goes to the network fee wherever it comes from, so kUSDT Wallet and the web app with KasWare do not send a swap, a deposit or a withdrawal that would leave the network more than 0.05 KAS of it beyond the fee shown, and say so; with Max the fee shown is the 0.3 KAS Max keeps. A site’s request whose amount falls in that band is refused in the approval window, and the site has to ask for another amount. Not held to it: the pool’s opening (the admin’s, whose screen shows no fee).
  • Funding comes only from plain Schnorr P2PK coins, each already in a block or made by the pool’s or the minter’s own last transaction.
  • kUSDT owned by a P2SH script or by another covenant can’t trade with the pool directly: the input that authorizes its owner is neither a plain coin nor one of the two families. It moves to a plain owner first.
  • New covenant ids: rc.22 needs a new kUSDT and a new pool. On the testnet, balances from before do not carry over.

What is done about it since round 11 (11 November 2026). The services, the wallet and the web app check every such transaction and never build behind one. The monitor shows it on /health. The monitor, the coordinator, the keeper and govern-submit send their own transaction again at a higher fee where that can replace it, and through extra Kaspa nodes the operators configure (kaspaSubmitRpcs): a node that builds blocks has already dropped it. The monitor raises pause-unlanded when its pauses reach a node and no block.

Since round 12 (11 November 2026, on rc.17):

  • Checking such a transaction costs the same whatever it holds. The check of round 11 asked the node for every coin at an address that the checked transaction itself named, once for each of its inputs that claimed an age, and the coordinator waited for it in the loop that follows mints and redemptions: one transaction written for the purpose could hold mints and redemptions up for as long as its sender kept it fresh. Now no address is read that a checked transaction names. An age is checked only for the coin the service or the client follows itself, a transaction that claims an age for any other coin counts as one no block can take, and the coordinator’s look at the mempool runs beside that loop with a deadline of 8 seconds.
  • Every mint goes to the extra Kaspa nodes from its first try, so that it is already waiting where blocks are built when a copy of it reaches the ordinary nodes. While a mint waits, the coordinator looks every few seconds at what spends the minter’s coin on its node, and tries again at once when a copy that no block can take has taken the mint’s place. It used to notice after two minutes.
  • A mint no longer takes the place of the guardian’s pause, or of a governance action, in a node’s mempool. It is sent plainly; when the node refuses it because another transaction spends the minter’s coin, the coordinator looks at that transaction and replaces it only if no block can take it or if it is the same mint. The monitor sends its next pause as soon as it sees that the coin its last pause was built on was spent by something else.

What is left:

  • With no such extra node configured, or none that reaches a miner, and a parked fee above the services’ bounds, the pause of the pool or the minter, a mint, a tick or a governance action waits until the parked transaction expires. The operators’ runbook has the steps. For mints this is not a rare case to plan for later: anyone can try it at every mint, for the fee of one plain transaction, by sending a copy of the mint with a fee above the coordinator’s bound (since round 13, twice the node’s estimate at that nonce’s first try; it was 1 600 sompi per gram, and the copy, whose fee is never paid, set what every mint paid up to that). The copy has to reach the nodes before a block takes the mint, which is a race of about a second and easier to win when the network is busy. A coordinator without a working extra node can then be kept from minting for up to 24 hours at a time. The extra nodes are a condition for minting through this, and the launch list treats them so.
  • The same copy can be made one that a block can take, funded by a coin of the sender’s own at a higher feerate, and then pushed out by a plain spend of that coin (round 13, R13-MINT-1). Nothing then spends the minter coin, and the mint is in no mempool; until round 13 the coordinator sent nothing again for two minutes, and minting stopped on both chains for each such pair, for a few hundredths of a KAS. Now the coordinator offers the mint to the extra nodes for as long as the copy holds the coin, and sends the same signed mint again at once when it finds nothing on the coin. What is left is a race per copy: the copy must take the mint’s place before a block takes the mint, and the plain spend the copy’s before a block takes the copy, and the mint is back within one look (3 seconds). A copy a block takes mints the same batch, so nothing is lost either way. The operator does nothing; a coordinator whose log keeps saying the same signed mint was sent again for one batch is being raced, and the extra nodes are what shortens it.
  • A transaction of someone else’s that spends the pool and puts an age on another of its inputs (its own funding coin, say) is treated as one no block can take, although a block may take it: the services and the clients cannot check that age without reading an address that transaction names. They do not build behind it for the seconds it waits, and a keeper’s tick or a pause may take its place in the mempool. No transaction of this project’s wallet, web app or services is built that way; other software that trades with the pool should not be either.
  • A wallet or a page judges a sale or a tick waiting in the mempool by the age of the confirmed pool, which it reads at the pool’s own address. When it has not found that coin (the coordinator named no confirmed pool, or named another), it does not build on that transaction’s output and asks again: a trade can take a few seconds longer to start in a busy pool. A token the wallet’s own waiting sale spends can show as still there for those seconds.
  • A governance action that waits two minutes in a node’s mempool without being accepted counts as one no block will take, like any spend that does (a spend parked with a short lock cannot be told apart from it). A mint may then take its place if it pays more per gram. The action keeps its signatures and is sent again.
  • A wallet whose node keeps such a transaction is told so and sends nothing; setting another node in Settings, or waiting, are the ways out. A transaction built behind one that the wallet could not recognise stays unconfirmed until the parked one is dropped; its funds are not lost.
  • The services see what spends a coin by asking the node for that coin’s address, and the node answers with every waiting transaction that pays the address as well (round 14, R14-ST-1). Anyone can fill that answer with a chain of transactions no block takes, each paying the address about 0.11 KAS, for one real fee: past 64 MB the answer is refused. Until round 14 the keeper then sent no tick for as long as the chain stood (it asked for its own address and the pool’s, and a new key’s address can be filled the same way), and the coordinator’s look at a waiting mint failed each time, so a mint whose place a copy had taken waited its 120 s again. Since round 14 the keeper asks for its own transactions by their ids and reads only the pool’s address; a read that fails, is refused or lists more than 16 transactions paying the pool is not made again for 30 s, and meanwhile its ticks go out plainly. The coordinator sends the same signed mint again at each look that fails, as when a look finds nothing. Both learn what holds the coin from the node’s refusal (a double spend names the one transaction that holds it) and judge that transaction by its id. What is left: while such a flood stands, a tick or a resent mint goes out at most a look after the coin is free, an honest spend on its way costs one refused tick, and a parked spend that also holds the coin is found one round later than before (a second at the keeper, a look at the coordinator). The operator does nothing; the keeper’s log says the node's mempool at the pool's address could not be read once when it starts and once when it ends. The monitor’s own looks at these addresses were already allowed to fail (its pause is then built without the mempool, as before), but while a coin’s address is flooded its /health does not show a parked spend of that coin; that was not changed in round 14. Not run on a node: the receiving side of the node’s answer was read in rusty-kaspa (mining/src/mempool/model/pool.rs).
  • The node rule was run on kaspad 2.1.0 by the outside review (12 November 2026, on rc.19): a spend with an age on an unconfirmed parent’s output was accepted, kept after the parent was mined, and could be replaced only at a higher feerate. It is to be reported to the node’s maintainers. What rc.22 closes rests on the node’s mark for an unconfirmed output (u64::MAX): a node version that changes that mark needs another look before the node pin moves.
  • What rc.22 does not change: a copy of a signed mint can still race the coordinator’s mint (a block can now take the copy, and it mints the same batch), a reorg that drops the block of a parent can still hold a spend back, and fees still decide which of two valid spends a block takes.

The pool can be paused

Section titled “The pool can be paused”

On any alarm the guardian pauses the pool with the rest of the bridge. Swaps and new liquidity stop until the pool admin resumes it. Removing liquidity works whatever the pause, unless a stolen admin key keeps moving the pool (below).

A stolen pool admin key can hold the pool up

Section titled “A stolen pool admin key can hold the pool up”

The pool admin key is kept offline. Whoever steals it can take the accrued fees, unpause the pool and hand the keys over, the guardian key included (after which no monitor can pause the pool; every monitor raises pool-keys-changed). It cannot touch the reserves or a position. What it can also do, for as long as its holder pays a network fee a spend, is keep every other pool transaction out, the withdrawals from a paused pool included: set_keys to the same two keys changes nothing but moves the pool to a new outpoint, and so do an unpause with a pause after it and a collect, so whatever was built on the outpoint before is a double spend. The admin entries do not read their input’s sequence either, so one admin spend of a pool output still in the mempool, with a sequence no block can meet, parks the pool coin in ordinary nodes for a day (as “A spend of the pool or the minter that no block can take” above). Round 11 closed the same thing for the guardian’s key, whose second pause of a paused pool is now refused; the admin’s entries were left as they were, and this covenant is not changed for it (found in round 13, R13-COV-L1). Providers get out between the thief’s spends, by paying more per gram than the thief, or through an extra node that reaches a miner.

What the operators do: with the admin key still in their hands too, send pool-set-keys (kusdt-tx) to a new admin key and the same guardian, built on each new pool output, at a high fee and to several nodes (kaspaSubmitRpcs), until one lands: from then on the stolen key can sign nothing. If the thief has already handed the admin key to itself, the pool stays usable only as far as the thief lets it: deploy a new pool, point the web app and the wallets at it, and tell the providers to withdraw from the old one as their wallets find a slot. The runbook has the steps (“The pool admin key is stolen”). Not decided, for a later pool covenant: unpause only on a paused pool and set_keys only with a change narrow this (the admin can still alternate keys); a least age between the admin’s spends closes it.

The first deposit sets the pool’s price

Section titled “The first deposit sets the pool’s price”

Whoever makes the first deposit into a new pool sets its starting price. Arbitrage then moves it toward the market.

A signer restored from an old backup

Section titled “A signer restored from an old backup”

A signer keeps a second record of what it signed, and of its daily limit, on storage of its own that a restore of the state does not touch. An old state file therefore forgets nothing, and the signer carries on with its limit intact.

If that storage is lost too, a signer that has signed before cannot know what it signed since its state file, so it holds itself: its daily limits count as spent for 24 hours. The others carry on without it.

What is left is a whole machine rolled back to a snapshot, which takes both records back together. A mint on the chain that carries the signer’s signature and that its records lack makes it hold itself; otherwise the operators’ restore steps set fuseHoldUntil by hand. A signer in that state still cannot mint alone: a mint takes 3 of the 5 signers, each with its own limit, and the minter’s on-chain allowance still applies.

Such a signer has forgotten for good, not for 24 hours, which mints it signed: fuseHoldUntil only delays it. Its refunds are not made weaker by that, since every refund waits the deepest finality tier whatever its size and whatever the signer remembers (since round 14, R14-ONCE-L1).

A signer keeps about a day of mints and a week of releases

Section titled “A signer keeps about a day of mints and a week of releases”

A signer’s records are held whole in memory and written whole at every save, so since round 16 (R16-ONCE-3) it lets go of what no signature needs any more. A mint record goes once the minter’s move past it is a day deep, and the signer then signs no mint below that nonce at all, whoever asks: a mint there could only be used after a reorg of more than a day. A release record goes a week after it was signed at the earliest, once every RPC of its vault shows the payout paid at one finalized block (the id redeemed and nothing queued under it: the vault never pays it again), and the burn record of that release with it. A payout that is only queued is not paid: the vault marks the id redeemed when it queues it, and a cancel clears that. So the records of a payout held in a vault paused for longer than a week, or cancelled to be reinstated, stay until it is paid, and it is signed again from them after a reinstate, even at a burn address too full to list (round 17, R17-PAY-1, R17-ONCE-1, R17-ST-1; up to rc.22 they went once the payout was queued). A cancelled payout that is never reinstated keeps its records for good, a few hundred bytes each, asked about once a day. A machine rolled back to a snapshot still holds its fuse for a mint it signed after the snapshot: that mint’s nonce is above where the snapshot’s records stopped. What is given up: after a reorg of more than a day, which the bridge does not provide for in any case, every signer refuses the deposits it moved back until operators look at it. The monitors still keep every mint they have seen (not changed in round 16).

A cancelled forged payout holds the honest one of its id

Section titled “A cancelled forged payout holds the honest one of its id”

The vault marks the id of a cancelled payout, not the payout: release refuses that redeemId, and refund that nonce, until the owner reinstates it. Three stolen signer keys can queue a forged payout under the id of a real burn or a real skip that is still waiting, ahead of the honest one, for gas alone. Once it is cancelled after the alarm, that burn or deposit is not paid until the Safe reinstates the id, which it does only after setSigners has run (before, the forged payout’s own signatures would queue it again): at least 48 hours after the rotation, plus the cancellers’ window. Nothing leaves the vault meanwhile; the USDT stays there as backing. kusdt cancel-plan writes the cancels only: the coordinator’s /health lists the ids of burns and skips it read that a cancel holds, and the runbook says how to reinstate them (round 16, R16-ONCE-2).

How fast a frozen node is noticed

Section titled “How fast a frozen node is noticed”

The monitor pauses everything failClosedMs (5 minutes by default) after a chain’s head stops moving, or after a Kaspa node stops keeping the network’s pace. For a chain, the last 60 seconds of that must hold over consecutive rounds, so that one stale answer from an RPC does not pause the bridge. It cannot be much shorter: a pause takes hours to undo, so a brief outage at an RPC provider must not cause one.

The preflight check holds failClosedMs to at most half a payout’s wait, which leaves the other half for the pause to land: 5 of the 10 minutes on mainnet. Since round 17 (R17-PAY-2) the time from the alarm to a mined pause (a round, the pauses and a block, 2.5 minutes) must fit in the wait as well, a mainnet monitor refuses to start on an announced setPayoutDelay that breaks either rule, and a running one says on /health when a vault’s wait, read every five minutes, no longer fits. That is a warning, not a pause: the owner sets the wait, behind its 48 hours.

The wallet’s keys are the same on test networks and on mainnet

Section titled “The wallet’s keys are the same on test networks and on mainnet”

kUSDT Wallet derives its Kaspa keys on one path (m/44'/111111'/0') on every network, as Kaspa NG, KasWare and Kaspium do, so a recovery phrase shows the same coins in each of them. A Kaspa signature names no network, and which network a node serves is the node’s own word. A public node that says “Testnet” and shows the mainnet coins of your keys as test coins could therefore have a plain send of “test KAS” move real KAS: the transaction is valid on mainnet. The node cannot choose who is paid; the real KAS goes to the same key of whoever you sent test KAS to. kUSDT, pool and bridge transactions are not exposed: each spends a covenant coin that exists on one network only, and the EVM side signs its chain ID.

What the wallet does: on Testnet with a node from the public node network, before it signs a plain KAS send it asks a mainnet node, on a connection of its own, whether the coins it is about to spend exist on mainnet. If they do, or if it cannot ask, nothing is signed. The Send review says that the keys are shared.

What is left: this is a second source, not a proof. It fails if the mainnet node asked lies too (the same operator, or a resolver of the public node network that hands out both). It is not asked with a node you set yourself (Settings), nor on the Local network: there the node is yours to trust. What you can do: keep test networks and real KAS on different recovery phrases, or set your own Testnet node in Settings. Keys of their own for test networks would close this for good; that changes every Testnet address, so it is planned with a testnet reset, not done under funds people hold now.

Someone at your unlocked computer

Section titled “Someone at your unlocked computer”

kUSDT Wallet keeps its settings in the browser. What decides where money goes is not taken from there on trust: the contracts and chains are built in, a change to a network’s servers counts only if this wallet saved it while unlocked, and a site is told the wallet’s own address. That holds against something that can only write the browser’s stored data. It does not hold against someone who sits at your unlocked computer and opens the browser’s developer tools on the wallet itself: they can do what the wallet can, and read the recovery phrase while the wallet is unlocked. Lock the computer, and keep the wallet’s auto-lock short.

The testnet runs on one host

Section titled “The testnet runs on one host”

The contracts are deployed on testnet (TN10, Sepolia and BSC testnet), and the public testnet is open at testnet.trestly.io. Its five signers, the coordinator and the monitor run on one test host, in isolated containers, so it is not the federation of five independent operators that mainnet needs (see Operations). The wallet extension has a testnet build to download and is not in any store. Several protections on this site (finality checks, signer-side rules, the monitor’s alarms) live in those services, so they are only as good as their deployment. Those services run an image from before the third and fourth outside reviews, so those fixes are not live in them yet.
