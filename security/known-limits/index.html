Known limits

The bridge is federated

Section titled “The bridge is federated”

Kaspa cannot verify Ethereum or BNB Chain, and they cannot verify Kaspa. In v1 a federation of 5 signers attests to deposits and burns, and 3 of them suffice. The threat model shows what a bad quorum could do; rate limits on both sides cap it.

Upgrade path: zero-knowledge light-client verification. Kaspa’s Toccata upgrade includes an opcode that verifies zero-knowledge proofs (OpZkPrecompile, KIP-16), and KIP-17 lists trustless bridges built on it as a use case. A future version could mint on Kaspa against a proof of the deposit, checked on-chain, instead of signer signatures. This is the planned direction, not part of v1, and it has no date.

It would cover one direction only. A proof checked on Kaspa replaces the signers’ word about deposits, so it removes the risk of unbacked mints. Releases are the other direction: the vaults on Ethereum and BNB Chain would have to verify Kaspa burns themselves, which needs a Kaspa light client on the EVM side, a separate and harder project. Until then, a release rests on 3 of 5 signers, capped by each vault’s release allowance (250 000 USDT at once, under 750 000 in any 24 hours). Every payout waits in the vault first, and the monitor pauses the vaults the moment it sees one queued with no burn behind it.

A reorg that flips a mint and a skip

Section titled “A reorg that flips a mint and a skip”

After a reorg that flips a mint and a skip, the coordinator and the monitor need an operator. Only the signers undo what the dropped blocks accepted. The coordinator and the monitor keep following a minter state that is gone, so they fail safe: the monitor raises minter-lost and pauses, and no signer signs a refund from such a view. An operator then brings them back from a recent checkpoint.

Blocked recipients

Section titled “Blocked recipients”

A blocked recipient is never paid. This was decided on 4 November 2026.

Ethereum USDT keeps a blacklist (isBlackListed(address)), edited by Tether. BSC-USD (0x55d398326f99059fF775485246999027B3197955) keeps none, and cannot be paused either. USDT’s blacklist stops only senders: transfer checks msg.sender, and transferFrom checks from. So the vault would still pay a blacklisted address, and the USDT would be frozen there for good. The bridge stops it before the vault:

  • Wallets ask first. Before every burn, the wallet and the web app call GET /recipients/:chain/:address and refuse an address the vault would not pay. If they cannot ask, they refuse too: a burn cannot be undone. The wallet asks again right before it sends, and neither offers the other chain for a blocked address.
  • Signers refuse. A signer will not sign a release or a refund to an address the token reports blocked. It asks every one of its RPCs, and signs nothing if they disagree. The deployment names the view to read (blocklist, per chain).
  • A burn that lands anyway is held. Its status is blocked. The coordinator checks it again every hour and pays it if the token unblocks the address. A refund to a blocked depositor waits the same way. A held record never holds up the others.

One gap: the vault can’t read a blocklist, and anyone may pay a queued payout. If the token blocks a recipient during the wait, the coordinator holds its payout, but anyone else could still pay it, and the USDT would be frozen there.

Nothing can send a held burn elsewhere. The burn commits to its address, and no key can redirect it: the vault has no re-target power. Its USDT stays in the vault as backing for as long as the address is blocked.

All this was checked on mainnet forks (npm run fork:rehearsal): a release to a blacklisted address is paid and frozen there, a blacklisted vault pays nothing, and BSC-USD keeps no blacklist. The held-then-paid flow for releases and refunds, the signers’ refusals and the vault-blocked pause were checked in npm run local:load.

Tether can block payouts

Section titled “Tether can block payouts”
Case Effect
The release recipient is blocked by the token See Blocked recipients
A vault’s own address is blacklisted by the token’s issuer That vault can pay nothing out, while deposits still go in. The monitor raises vault-blocked and the guardian pauses everything (The monitor)
Ethereum USDT turns on its dormant transfer fee (basisPointsRate) Deposits revert (the vault checks the exact amount received), and release recipients receive less than the payout

A burn’s payload can be missing

Section titled “A burn’s payload can be missing”

A redemption transaction should carry the burn’s preimage in its payload. If it does not, the burn is still valid, but the bridge cannot know its chain and address from the chain alone. Anyone can give the coordinator the preimage later (POST /redeems), and it is checked against the burn owner. Until then, the burn waits.

Redemptions depend on one vault

Section titled “Redemptions depend on one vault”

kUSDT is one fungible token, whichever chain its USDT came from. A release, however, is paid by one vault:

  • A release must not exceed that vault’s backing. kUSDT minted from Ethereum deposits can be redeemed on BNB Chain only if the BNB Chain vault holds enough.
  • A single burn output, fee included, must fit in that vault’s release capacity, 250 000 USDT by default.

Wallets refuse a burn the named vault cannot pay now (above its release capacity or releaseAvailable, above its backing, or while it is paused) and offer the other chain. If one lands anyway, it waits: the relayer retries it with its own backoff until the allowance refills, deposits on that chain bring the backing, or the owner raises the capacity within its hard bound. It never holds up other burns.

Rebalancing uses the bridge’s own paths. No key can move backing from one vault to the other. When one chain runs short, an operator holding kUSDT redeems it from the vault that has plenty, moves the USDT across (an exchange or another bridge), and deposits it into the short vault, which mints the same kUSDT back. Backing moves for 0.5 % each way plus the cost of the crossing, and no new trust is added. See Operations.

kUSDT is backed by the two vaults together. If one chain’s stablecoin depegs or is mis-minted, kUSDT minted from it can redeem real USDT from the other vault, up to the release allowances. Nothing on either chain can tell which vault a token came from, and the backing check (INV-1) counts both vaults as one. In a run on one chain, holders are paid on the other chain only while that vault holds enough. The monitor warns on /health when one chain’s releases exceed what its deposits minted by more than releaseCapacity: that is a warning, and it pauses nothing, because moving value across chains is a normal use.

USDT sent straight to a vault is lost

Section titled “USDT sent straight to a vault is lost”

Only the vault’s deposit function bridges. USDT sent to a vault’s address with a plain transfer (from an exchange withdrawal, say) names no Kaspa address and gets no deposit number: nothing is minted for it, and no function of the vault can send it back. It adds to the backing and stays there. The vault has no owner withdrawal of anything but fees, on purpose: such a power would be a way to take the backing. Always bridge through the web app or the wallet.

The daily limit can be used up by one party

Section titled “The daily limit can be used up by one party”

The signers sign for a limited amount a day, across both chains (25 000 USDT at the launch), for mints and for payouts each. Someone who uses that up keeps other people waiting until the day has passed: a deposit made then waits, and is minted when there is room again. Nothing is lost. Raising the limit only raises the money such a party needs.

Deposits: the fee rises with use. Decided on 10 November 2026. Each vault’s deposit fee goes up as the day’s deposits on it grow: 0.50 % until its deposit meter is half full, rising in a straight line to 2.50 % at a full meter, and 2.50 % while it stays full (Fees). Each vault’s meter is half the signers’ limit, so filling the limit fills both meters. At the launch values, filling both meters from empty costs 250 USDT in deposit fees on 25 000 USDT, against 125 at the flat rate, and anything deposited while a meter is full pays 2.50 %. This makes the attack cost more. It does not rule it out: a meter drains over a day, so a party willing to pay can do it again the next day. Honest depositors pay the higher rate too while a meter is more than half full, and the web app says so.

Bridging back: still the flat fee. The rising fee covers deposits only. A release’s fee is set by the vault after the kUSDT is burnt, and a burn cannot be undone, so nobody could refuse a higher fee at that point. So someone holding kUSDT can still use up the signers’ limit for payouts by bridging back that much, for the flat fee of 0.50 % (125 USDT on 25 000 at the launch). Other people’s payouts then wait, up to a day. Nothing is lost. This part is open.

Small burns are not paid

Section titled “Small burns are not paid”

A burn whose amount is at or below the release fee (5 USDT on Ethereum, 0.50 USDT on BNB Chain, or 0.50 % if higher) is not paid. It is marked unpayable, and the tokens are still burned. It is looked at again about once a minute and paid if the fee falls below its amount; nothing obliges the owner to lower the fee. A burn naming the zero address, the vault itself or the token contract’s own address is lost for good.

The wallet and the web app refuse such a burn, and warn when the fee would take more than 20 % of a redemption. Only a transaction built by hand can still lose a burn this way. Each burn output also locks 0.3 KAS for good (below).

Only plain burns are paid

Section titled “Only plain burns are paid”

Signers refuse a burn state whose borrow scheme, borrow guard or extension commitment is not zero, because a borrowable state could move to a new outpoint and be claimed twice. Such kUSDT is lost. Wallets never build one. The coordinator does not record such a state as a redemption at all, nor one with an amount of zero.

Burns lock KAS too

Section titled “Burns lock KAS too”

An ordinary kUSDT state carries the deployment’s token value, 1.5 KAS, for its storage mass, and that KAS comes back when the state is spent. A burn state can never be spent, so it carries the smaller burn value, 0.3 KAS, which is gone for good. Wallets show it as part of a redemption’s cost.

Rules the vault cannot check

Section titled “Rules the vault cannot check”

The vault cannot see Kaspa, and USDT pays a blacklisted address. Four rules rest on the signers alone:

  • a release pays the chain and address the burn owner commits to;
  • a refund pays the depositor;
  • nothing is paid to an address the token blocks;
  • a nonce is never both minted and refunded (INV-2).

A bad quorum that broke them would still be capped by the release allowance.

A governance action credits the mint allowance only up to the age its signers signed

Section titled “A governance action credits the mint allowance only up to the age its signers signed”

The mint allowance refills with the age of the minter’s own UTXO, and every spend of the minter starts that age again. A mint signs the age it claims, so nobody can change it. Since rc.16 a governance action (an unpause, a change of limits or signers, a skip, a recovery proposal or veto) signs its age too. Before rc.16 it did not, and anyone who saw a governance transaction before it was accepted could send a copy that claimed a smaller age and threw the built-up allowance away. That is closed: a copy that claims another age fails the signature check.

What remains is smaller:

  • The time after the signed age is not credited. The signers sign an age a little below the UTXO’s age when they sign (the tools leave a margin of 600 DAA, one minute), and the action lands later. The allowance that refills in between, minutes to hours of it, is not credited. When the signed age already covers a full refill, nothing is left out.
  • An action signed for age 0 credits no refill. A rotation of the signers, a recovery proposal and a veto are signed for age 0 unless the signers say otherwise, and any other action can be: a signed age is also a wait, because a transaction is valid only once the minter UTXO is that old, and a spend of the minter between signing and sending makes a new UTXO at age zero. After an action at age 0, a large deposit can wait for the refill, up to a day for a full allowance.
  • An action signed for an age above 0 can be delayed. Each spend of the minter starts the UTXO’s age again, and while spends keep landing closer together than the age signed, the action cannot be sent. It is not voided. The spends can come from outside the team: every deposit leads to a mint and anyone can make a deposit (it costs a deposit each time), and whoever holds three signer keys can mint one unit at a time, or propose and veto a recovery in turn. That is why the actions that answer a theft of keys default to age 0, which nothing can delay. For the others (an unpause, new limits, a skip, the minter’s deposit) the signers pass --age 0 when mints are flowing. Holding mints on the coordinator stops only the coordinator’s own new mints.
  • A guardian pause credits nothing for the time before it, and a recovery carried out credits nothing for its 72-hour wait. Those two steps claim no age at all: a pause must never wait on a clock, and a recovery has no signers to sign an age.

In each case mints wait until the allowance has refilled. Nothing is minted, moved or lost.

The coordinator keeps everything in memory

Section titled “The coordinator keeps everything in memory”

For now the coordinator keeps every record it ever made (deposits, redemptions, refunds) in one state file, and holds that file and its whole index of kUSDT outputs in memory. It has no archive for closed records yet. Both grow with use and never shrink. Someone could grow them on purpose: by the project’s own estimate, about 250 000 real burns of one base unit each would exhaust its memory, at a cost of some 100 000 KAS that the attacker loses for good (each burn locks its KAS). That estimate was not measured on a running coordinator. The coordinator would then stop, and with it mints and payouts, until an operator steps in. No funds are at risk: the coordinator holds no key that moves them. Since rc.16 it no longer records burns that could never be paid (empty ones, and states with borrowing or an extension), which were the cheap way to do this. Moving closed records and the index to disk is to be done before mainnet.

The monitor has a smaller form of the same limit: it keeps a record of every real burn for good. A record of an output that only looked like a burn is dropped once that output is spent.

An address that holds too many coins cannot be listed

Section titled “An address that holds too many coins cannot be listed”

A Kaspa node (kaspad 2.1.0) answers the coins at an address all at once or not at all. It has no call that lists them a page at a time, and none that answers about one output. Anyone can send an address coins. The services take at most 100 MB of such an answer, about 246,000 coins, and refuse a longer one before any of it is in memory. So an address that holds more can no longer be read by them, for as long as the coins stay there. Filling one costs whoever does it the coins for good: by the project’s own arithmetic at least 0.1 KAS a coin, some 25,000 KAS, and hours of the network’s block space. That was not measured on a network.

Since round 11 this no longer stops a payout or pauses the bridge:

  • A burn at such an address is still paid. The address of a burn is fixed by its amount, chain and recipient, and nothing can ever be spent from it, so coins sent there stay for good. Each signer and the coordinator also keep the burns their own followers read from the transactions the chain accepted, with the block that accepted each, and take a burn from that record when its address cannot be listed. A signer still signs only for a burn that is final on its own node, and checks on the spot that the block is still on its node’s chain.
  • The minter and the pool are still followed. A monitor that cannot list the minter’s or the pool’s own address says so on /health, with the address, and pauses nothing. It follows both through every accepted transaction as before.

What is left, until a kaspad release has a paged read:

  • A signer that never read the burn’s block has only the UTXO set. That is a signer started from a checkpoint taken after the burn, one that went on from a checkpoint after more than about 30 hours away, or one whose book of 20,000 records was full of larger outputs. For a burn at a filled address it refuses, and never guesses. Three signers that did read the block are enough. Operators keep it that way by restoring a signer from its state backup (its records come with it) instead of starting it from a fresh checkpoint while burns wait unpaid.
  • A monitor that never read the burn’s block fails closed. A monitor rebuilt from a checkpoint looks for an older burn in the UTXO set when its release is queued. If that address cannot be read for two minutes it raises unverifiable and everything is paused: with three stolen keys an attacker chooses the address a forged burn is looked for at, so “cannot read” must not pass. The operators’ steps are in the runbook.
  • A refund is not signed while the minter rests at a filled address. A signer proves its view of the minter by finding it in the UTXO set. The minter moves to a new address with any mint or governance action, and refunds are signed again.
  • A service’s own address. The coordinator’s funding address and a monitor’s guardian address are read whole only once (the services keep their own coins from then on). If one is filled before that, its operator compounds the coins into a few with the key.
  • What a wallet is shown. GET /tokens and GET /positions find an owner’s outputs in the UTXO set. While one of them sits at a filled address, that owner’s list cannot be answered. The outputs are still there, and still spendable by a wallet that knows their state.
  • A burn without a payload, on a coordinator that did not read its block, is confirmed only from the UTXO set. At a filled address its claim comes to nothing there. Wallets always attach the payload.

The public answers come from the processes that move and guard the money

Section titled “The public answers come from the processes that move and guard the money”

The coordinator’s API and the monitor’s status are answered by the same process, on the same thread, as the loops that mint and pay and the rounds that judge and pause. Since rc.17 what a request can cost is bounded: each address gets 240 requests a minute, the monitor builds each of its answers once a second however many ask, and the coordinator’s dear reads (an owner’s outputs at an address holding very many coins, lists from its archive) are shared, kept, and held to a budget of time. Under such requests a wallet can be shown a list of its outputs that is up to a minute old (marked stale), or be told to ask again (retryAt); mints, payouts and the monitor’s rounds keep their share.

What is left is a flood from very many addresses at once. No count per address stops that; every request still costs its process the handling of it, and a monitor that cannot finish its rounds for five minutes fails closed and pauses everything. It is a stall, with no funds at risk. What an operator does: keep the monitor’s address out of public view where it can be (serve the status from another machine that asks the monitor), put the coordinator’s API behind a provider that absorbs floods, and keep apiClientIp set as the preflight asks. The status is not yet served from a copy outside the judging process.

A coordinator’s read of an owner’s address still has the node build the whole list of coins there (the node, kaspad 2.1.0, has nothing narrower): the bounds above limit how often that happens, not what one read costs the node.

Pool contention

Section titled “Pool contention”

Each swap spends one exact pool state. When several users swap at once, all but one must re-quote and re-sign. This is the price of being immune to sandwich attacks. The wallet goes ahead on its own only when the new quote is at least as good as the one you confirmed; a worse one sends nothing and asks you again.

A node that takes a pool transaction has not accepted it: of two transactions spending the same pool state, each taken by a different node, only one is ever accepted. The wallet therefore waits (up to 30 seconds) to see which it was. It says “Swapped”, “Added” or “Removed” only once its node shows the transaction accepted. If another transaction won, it frees the kUSDT and coins the lost one had set aside at once and goes again on the new pool state under the rule above. If neither is shown in time, it says “sent, not confirmed yet”, keeps what the transaction spends set aside, and looks again at every refresh; Activity then says “did not go through” or drops the mark. What is left: the wallet learns this from one node. A node that is behind, or lies, can delay the answer or give a wrong one. A wrong “lost” cannot make a swap happen twice (the next try must spend one of the same coins or the same kUSDT state, so it is void if the first was accepted), but the wallet’s words about the first try are then wrong until the next refresh shows the coins gone.

The pool caps sells

Section titled “The pool caps sells”

The net KAS sells take out of the pool, less what buys bring in, may be at most 10 % of its KAS reserve per hour (the circuit breaker). It bounds a dump of forged kUSDT, but it also slows a large honest sell, which must wait for buys or the next window. Buys are not limited. Because it counts the net flow, round trips (a buy, then a sell back) cannot use up the room; until 5 November 2026 they could.

Pool transactions wait a few seconds while more than half of the cap is in use

Section titled “Pool transactions wait a few seconds while more than half of the cap is in use”

The sell cap refills with the age of the pool’s own UTXO, and time a pool transaction does not claim is lost. So while sells have used more than half of the cap, every pool transaction (a swap, a deposit, a withdrawal) must claim at least 50 DAA of that age, about 5 seconds: the pool can’t be spent more often than that, and each spend refills at least that much. The wallet and the web app wait for it on their own and send at that moment, and a transaction right after another pool transaction can take a few seconds longer. At or below half of the cap there is no wait, and a withdrawal while the pool is paused never waits (the cap’s clock stands still during a pause). Withdrawals are never refused for it.

Each pool state then makes one slot every 5 seconds, which goes to whichever transaction the network takes first. A bot that always gets there first can keep every other pool transaction out while more than half of the cap is in use: for at most half an hour after a full cap was sold (an hour and a half if half the pool was also withdrawn), unless it also keeps selling about a whole cap an hour. A seller who wins one race in two waits a slot or two.

A bot can also let the pool’s UTXO age and then spend it claiming only those 5 seconds, so the cap refills slowly. The operators run a keeper against this: once it sees such a spend, it settles the cap’s clock in each slot for the next hour, 2.5 seconds after the wallets would, so a bot that lands late no longer lands at all. One that lands within those 2.5 seconds wastes that much of each slot, and one that stays under the keeper’s 5 second threshold is never caught and can halve the refill while nobody else trades. Either way no KAS leaves. Anyone may run a keeper.

A pool so small that its cap is under 720 sompi (under 0.0001 KAS of KAS reserve) refills nothing on the shortest claim, because the refill rounds down. The pool’s first deposit takes its admin’s signature, and so does the first deposit after every provider has left (which starts the cap again at the new reserve), so nobody can open it with dust. One case is left: if the last provider leaves while someone keeps a dust position in the pool, it is not empty, anyone may add to it, and its cap stays the dust’s; a bot spending every slot can then keep it from growing, and a pause and a fresh start by the admin are the way out.

The admin’s deposit into a pool that every provider has left is signed for one pool state, and the admin key is kept offline. While that pool is open anyone can move it to a new state for a small fee (a tick every 5 seconds, or a dust swap), and a deposit carried over from the offline computer then no longer fits. No money is at risk, and it costs whoever does it a fee each time. The operators’ way through: the guardian pauses the pool, which nobody but the admin can then move, and the admin sends the unpause and the deposit together. A pool that was never opened can’t be moved this way at all: until its first deposit only the admin and the guardian can spend it.

A spend of the pool or the minter that no block can take

Section titled “A spend of the pool or the minter that no block can take”

A Kaspa node accepts a transaction that spends an output still waiting in its mempool without checking the age that transaction claims for it. A block does check. So a transaction can be made that ordinary nodes keep for up to 24 hours and that no block ever includes. If it spends the pool’s coin, those nodes refuse every other pool transaction as a double spend for as long as they keep it. Anyone can do this to the pool, at any time, for the fee of one plain transaction, and again when it expires. The same can be done to the minter’s coin by whoever holds a signed minter transaction that is not in a block yet (the coordinator, or a holder of three signer keys), and by anyone while a recovery proposal stands.

What it costs while it stands: swaps, deposits and withdrawals of liquidity sent through ordinary nodes are refused or stay unconfirmed, mints wait, and a pause of the pool or the minter, or a veto, sent through an ordinary node does not land. Nothing is taken by it, the vaults’ pauses on Ethereum and BNB Chain do not depend on it, and it ends as soon as any real spend of that coin reaches a block.

What is done about it since round 11 (11 November 2026). The services, the wallet and the web app check every such transaction and never build behind one. The monitor shows it on /health. The monitor, the coordinator, the keeper and govern-submit send their own transaction again at a higher fee where that can replace it, and through extra Kaspa nodes the operators configure (kaspaSubmitRpcs): a node that builds blocks has already dropped it. The monitor raises pause-unlanded when its pauses reach a node and no block.

What is left:

  • With no such extra node configured, or none that reaches a miner, and a parked fee above the services’ bounds, the pause of the pool or the minter, a mint, a tick or a governance action waits until the parked transaction expires. The operators’ runbook has the steps.
  • A wallet whose node keeps such a transaction is told so and sends nothing; setting another node in Settings, or waiting, are the ways out. A transaction built behind one that the wallet could not recognise stays unconfirmed until the parked one is dropped; its funds are not lost.
  • All of this rests on reading the Kaspa node’s source (rusty-kaspa v2.1.0). It has not been run on a node. The node rule itself (the age check on a spend of an unconfirmed output) is to be reported to the node’s maintainers.
  • The next covenant version is planned to close the minter’s side (a signed minter transaction bound to its submitter). The pool’s entries are public by design and stay open to it.

The pool can be paused

Section titled “The pool can be paused”

On any alarm the guardian pauses the pool with the rest of the bridge. Swaps and new liquidity stop until the pool admin resumes it. Removing liquidity always works.

The first deposit sets the pool’s price

Section titled “The first deposit sets the pool’s price”

Whoever makes the first deposit into a new pool sets its starting price. Arbitrage then moves it toward the market.

A signer restored from an old backup

Section titled “A signer restored from an old backup”

A signer keeps a second record of what it signed, and of its daily limit, on storage of its own that a restore of the state does not touch. An old state file therefore forgets nothing, and the signer carries on with its limit intact.

If that storage is lost too, a signer that has signed before cannot know what it signed since its state file, so it holds itself: its daily limits count as spent for 24 hours. The others carry on without it.

What is left is a whole machine rolled back to a snapshot, which takes both records back together. A mint on the chain that carries the signer’s signature and that its records lack makes it hold itself; otherwise the operators’ restore steps set fuseHoldUntil by hand. A signer in that state still cannot mint alone: a mint takes 3 of the 5 signers, each with its own limit, and the minter’s on-chain allowance still applies.

How fast a frozen node is noticed

Section titled “How fast a frozen node is noticed”

The monitor pauses everything failClosedMs (5 minutes by default) after a chain’s head stops moving, or after a Kaspa node stops keeping the network’s pace. For a chain, the last 60 seconds of that must hold over consecutive rounds, so that one stale answer from an RPC does not pause the bridge. It cannot be much shorter: a pause takes hours to undo, so a brief outage at an RPC provider must not cause one.

The preflight check holds failClosedMs to at most half a payout’s wait, which leaves the other half for the pause to land: 5 of the 10 minutes on mainnet.

The wallet’s keys are the same on test networks and on mainnet

Section titled “The wallet’s keys are the same on test networks and on mainnet”

kUSDT Wallet derives its Kaspa keys on one path (m/44'/111111'/0') on every network, as Kaspa NG, KasWare and Kaspium do, so a recovery phrase shows the same coins in each of them. A Kaspa signature names no network, and which network a node serves is the node’s own word. A public node that says “Testnet” and shows the mainnet coins of your keys as test coins could therefore have a plain send of “test KAS” move real KAS: the transaction is valid on mainnet. The node cannot choose who is paid; the real KAS goes to the same key of whoever you sent test KAS to. kUSDT, pool and bridge transactions are not exposed: each spends a covenant coin that exists on one network only, and the EVM side signs its chain ID.

What the wallet does: on Testnet with a node from the public node network, before it signs a plain KAS send it asks a mainnet node, on a connection of its own, whether the coins it is about to spend exist on mainnet. If they do, or if it cannot ask, nothing is signed. The Send review says that the keys are shared.

What is left: this is a second source, not a proof. It fails if the mainnet node asked lies too (the same operator, or a resolver of the public node network that hands out both). It is not asked with a node you set yourself (Settings), nor on the Local network: there the node is yours to trust. What you can do: keep test networks and real KAS on different recovery phrases, or set your own Testnet node in Settings. Keys of their own for test networks would close this for good; that changes every Testnet address, so it is planned with a testnet reset, not done under funds people hold now.

Someone at your unlocked computer

Section titled “Someone at your unlocked computer”

kUSDT Wallet keeps its settings in the browser. What decides where money goes is not taken from there on trust: the contracts and chains are built in, a change to a network’s servers counts only if this wallet saved it while unlocked, and a site is told the wallet’s own address. That holds against something that can only write the browser’s stored data. It does not hold against someone who sits at your unlocked computer and opens the browser’s developer tools on the wallet itself: they can do what the wallet can, and read the recovery phrase while the wallet is unlocked. Lock the computer, and keep the wallet’s auto-lock short.

The testnet runs on one host

Section titled “The testnet runs on one host”

The contracts are deployed on testnet (TN10, Sepolia and BSC testnet), and the public testnet is open at testnet.trestly.io. Its five signers, the coordinator and the monitor run on one test host, in isolated containers, so it is not the federation of five independent operators that mainnet needs (see Operations). The wallet extension has a testnet build to download and is not in any store. Several protections on this site (finality checks, signer-side rules, the monitor’s alarms) live in those services, so they are only as good as their deployment. Those services run an image from before the third and fourth outside reviews, so those fixes are not live in them yet.
